## The Shadow AI Problem Nobody Is Tracking

"Shadow AI" is what happens when employees use AI tools their company never approved, with data their company never authorized. It's not a fringe problem — it's the dominant risk in business AI adoption today.

The pattern is always the same:

- Someone discovers a free AI tool that saves them 20 minutes
- They use it with **real** customer, patient, or client data
- Nobody in leadership knows it's happening
- The tool's terms may allow training on inputs — meaning that data can resurface in someone else's query

Here's what that means in practice. If an AI provider trains on your inputs and doesn't offer non-retention, your customer's personal information becomes part of the model. You can't recall it. You can't delete it. You can't even verify who else might eventually see it.

For a business in Metro Detroit, that's not an abstract IT concern. That's a liability with a street address.

## HIPAA: Where Free AI Becomes a Compliance Violation

If you're a covered entity — or a business associate of one — protected health information (PHI) has strict rules around use and disclosure. **Pasting PHI into a free consumer AI tool violates HIPAA.** No interpretation needed.

The industry-standard safeguard is a Business Associate Agreement (BAA). A BAA is a contract between a covered entity and any vendor that handles PHI, spelling out exactly how the data can be used, stored, and protected. When you evaluate an AI provider, the first question is always:

> "Will you sign our BAA?"

A "no" — or a hesitant "maybe" — is an immediate deal-breaker.

But here's what most guides skip: a BAA covers a *vendor's* handling of your data. It does not stop the data from leaving your building in the first place. For a healthcare practice that treats privacy as non-negotiable, the more elegant answer is to never send the data anywhere — which is exactly what **private AI that stays on-premises** makes possible.

The stakes aren't hypothetical. Fines, license risk, and reputational damage are the documented consequences for practices that got this wrong. And with state enforcement activity rising sharply since 2025, the odds of "nobody checks" get worse every quarter.

## Attorney-Client Privilege: The Risk No Law Firm Can Afford

For law firms, the stakes are even sharper: **sharing client information or case details with an AI tool can waive attorney-client privilege.**

Privilege is the foundation of legal practice. Once client communications are disclosed to a third party — and a public AI provider absolutely counts as a third party — the client's expectation of confidentiality is compromised. That can blow up a case, trigger ethics complaints, and destroy a firm's reputation in one afternoon.

The pattern we see in the legal community:

- A paralegal uses a general chatbot to summarize a client's medical narrative
- The tool has no confidentiality protections and no BAA
- The client's diagnosis, treatment history, and identifiers are now in the model's training data

The safest architecture for a firm that handles sensitive client work is not a "more careful" cloud tool. It's a **local LLM** — an AI system running on hardware the firm owns, inside the firm's own walls, that never touches the public internet.

## Proprietary Data: Trade Secrets, Pricing, and the Quiet Leak

HIPAA and privilege get the headlines. But the most common shadow AI leak in Metro Detroit is plain old proprietary information:

- Pricing sheets and margins
- Proprietary processes and formulations
- Customer lists and contract terms
- R&D notes and product plans

Once that goes into a public AI tool, it's no longer a secret you control. And unlike a stolen laptop, you'll never know it happened — no alarm, no missing file, just a competitor's chatbot that somehow "knows" your pricing strategy.

## The Compliance Checklist for 2026

Before your team uses any AI tool with business data, run this checklist:

1. **Inventory the tools.** Walk the office. Which AI tools are actually in use — approved or not?
2. **Categorize the data.** Which of your data is PHI, privileged, or proprietary? Those categories never go into public tools. Period.
3. **Ask the BAA question.** For cloud AI that touches PHI, require a signed BAA and non-retention terms.
4. **Match the architecture to the data.** Public cloud AI is fine for low-sensitivity work. For regulated data, the answer is on-premises.
5. **Write the policy.** A one-page AI usage policy — what's allowed, what's forbidden, who owns enforcement. Then train the team on it.
6. **Plan for enforcement.** State regulators now require documented risk assessments and, for some businesses, audits. Your policy is your evidence.

## FAQ

### What is "shadow AI" and why is it risky?
Shadow AI is the use of AI tools by employees without company approval or awareness. It's risky because sensitive data — customer information, PHI, privileged communications, trade secrets — can be sent to third-party tools that may train on it, creating compliance violations and data exposure the business never authorized.

### What data should never be entered into AI tools?
Social Security numbers and personal identifiers, protected health information (PHI) covered by HIPAA, attorney-client privileged communications, and proprietary business information such as pricing, processes, and trade secrets.

### Can a business use AI with patient data and stay HIPAA compliant?
Yes — but only with HIPAA-compliant AI that includes a signed Business Associate Agreement (BAA), encryption at rest and in transit, and documented breach notification procedures. The safest option is on-premises AI where patient data never leaves the business.

### How does using public AI tools risk attorney-client privilege?
Sharing client information or case details with a third-party AI tool can waive attorney-client privilege, because the client's expectation of confidentiality is compromised the moment the data is disclosed to an outside provider without adequate protections.

### What is a local LLM device?
A local LLM device is a self-contained AI system that runs on hardware you own, on your premises. Models execute locally and your data never leaves the building — eliminating the third-party exposure, training-data, and BAA dependency of cloud AI.

### Does on-premises AI require a Business Associate Agreement?
In most cases, no. A BAA is required when a vendor handles PHI on your behalf. When your own AI runs entirely on your own hardware with no vendor processing your data, the BAA requirement drops away — which is precisely why local LLM devices simplify compliance.
